Classic models

Even in LLM-era interviews, these come up every time: "why logistic and not linear regression?", "how does a tree choose a split?", "bagging vs boosting?", "what does C do in an SVM?", "why does k-NN break in high dimensions?". Each section lets you poke the model until the answer is obvious.

Concept

Logistic regression = a linear score squashed into a probability Must-know

\( p(y=1\mid x)=\sigma(w^\top x+b)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(w^\top x+b)}} \qquad L=-\frac1n\sum\big[y\log p+(1-y)\log(1-p)\big] \)

Linear regression on 0/1 labels predicts values outside [0, 1] and is dragged around by points far from the boundary, because squared error penalises "too correct" predictions. Logistic regression outputs a probability, its log-odds are linear in x (so coefficients are interpretable as odds ratios), and the log-loss is convex. Add a far-away positive and compare the two decision points.

Concept

A tree greedily picks the split that makes children purest Must-know

\( \text{Gini}=1-\sum_k p_k^2 \qquad \text{Entropy}=-\sum_k p_k\log_2 p_k \qquad \text{Gain}=I(\text{parent})-\sum_{c}\tfrac{n_c}{n}I(c) \)

At each node it tries every feature and threshold, and keeps the split with the biggest impurity decrease; then recurses. Trees need no scaling, handle non-linearity and interactions, and are easy to explain — but a fully grown tree memorises the data (high variance). Control it with max depth, min samples per leaf, pruning — or average many trees.

Concept

Bagging averages away variance; boosting adds up to remove bias Must-know

Bagging (random forest) trains deep, high-variance trees in parallel on bootstrap samples (plus random feature subsets) and averages them — errors that disagree cancel. Boosting (AdaBoost, gradient boosting, XGBoost, LightGBM) trains shallow, high-bias trees sequentially, each one fitting what the ensemble still gets wrong (the residuals / negative gradient), scaled by a learning rate. Boosting is usually more accurate on tabular data but easier to overfit and needs tuning; random forests are robust defaults.

Concept

Maximise the margin; C sets how much you pay for violations

\( \min_{w,b}\ \tfrac12\|w\|^2 + C\sum_i \max\big(0,\,1-y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\big) \)

An SVM finds the boundary with the widest gap to the nearest points; only those points — the support vectors — determine it. Large C: violations are expensive → narrow margin that bends to every point (low bias, high variance). Small C: tolerate violations → wide, smoother margin. The kernel trick replaces dot products with \(K(x,x')\) (RBF, polynomial) to get non-linear boundaries without computing the high-dimensional features.

Concept

k-NN: no training, vote among neighbours

Store the data; predict by majority vote (or average) of the k closest points. k = 1 memorises — a jagged boundary, high variance. Large k smooths — eventually predicts the majority class everywhere, high bias. It needs scaled features (distance!), is slow at prediction time (use ANN indexes), and struggles in high dimensions.

The curse of dimensionality Must-know

As dimensions grow, data becomes sparse and distances concentrate: the nearest and farthest points end up almost equally far away, so "nearest" stops meaning anything. You need exponentially more data to cover the space. Remedies: feature selection, PCA/embeddings, regularization, and models that don't rely on raw distances.
