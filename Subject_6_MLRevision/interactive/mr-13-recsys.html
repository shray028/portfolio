MODULE 13 · RECOMMENDERS

Recommender systems

"Design a product recommendation system" is the most common ML design question at e-commerce, ads and content companies. The answer has a standard shape — retrieve candidates, filter, rank, re-rank — and the follow-ups probe labels, negatives, cold start, combining objectives, evaluation and exploration. Examples here use an eyewear store app with physical stores.

Concept

Retrieve cheaply from millions, rank carefully over hundreds Must-know

Candidate generation pulls ~hundreds to a few thousand items from several sources in parallel (two-tower ANN on embeddings, co-viewed/co-bought, similar to recently viewed, trending in the user's city) — optimised for recall and speed. Filtering applies hard constraints: in stock (online or at the chosen store), deliverable, not already bought, policy rules. Ranking scores each remaining candidate with a richer model (GBDT or deep model predicting click, try-on, purchase) using user, item, context and cross features. Re-ranking applies diversity, freshness, business rules and exploration to the final slate. A strong v1: popularity by segment + co-purchase + in-stock filter + simple GBDT ranker.

Questions
Concept

Collaborative vs content-based vs hybrid Must-know

Collaborative filtering learns from behaviour alone: users who liked what you liked also liked X. Matrix factorisation represents each user and item as a vector so that their dot product predicts the interaction; it finds taste dimensions nobody labelled, but can't handle new users or items. Content-based uses item attributes (frame shape, material, colour, price, image embeddings) and the user's own history — works for new items, can be narrow. Hybrid / two-tower: a user tower (history, context, demographics) and an item tower (ID + attributes + image) trained so matching pairs score high — attributes rescue cold items, embeddings enable ANN retrieval.

Cold start Must-know

New user: popular and trending items for their segment (city, device, entry page), short onboarding preferences (face shape, style, budget), session behaviour updated within the visit (clicks, try-ons), and bandit exploration. New item: content features and visual embeddings place it near similar items in the item tower; boost or reserve exploration slots so it can earn interactions; borrow signals from the same brand/collection. Always keep a sensible fallback that respects inventory.

Concept

"Not clicked" is not the same as "not liked" Must-know

Interactions form a ladder of intent: impression → click → try-on → add to cart → purchase → kept (not returned). Higher rungs are more valuable but rarer; many systems train multi-task heads on several rungs and combine them. A negative needs exposure: an item shown but ignored is weak evidence of disinterest (and depends on position); an item the user never saw tells you nothing — sampled "random negatives" are a convenience for retrieval training, not truth. Watch for leakage: features computed after the impression, same-session future events, or using the label window in aggregates.

Concept

Constraints filter; objectives score; scales must be comparable Must-know

Several signals compete: predicted purchase probability, a fit/compatibility score (e.g. from a face-shape model), popularity, margin, availability. Treat hard constraints (out of stock at the user's store, not deliverable, wrong prescription type) as filters, not features — a high score must never surface an unavailable item. Combine soft signals on a common scale: calibrated probabilities, or normalise (rank/z-score) first, otherwise the signal with the biggest raw numbers (popularity counts) silently dominates. Hand-set weights are a fine v1; learn the trade-off later by training the ranker on the business outcome (purchases net of returns) with the signals as features, and tune weights by A/B test.

Concept

Recommenders create their own training data — so they get stuck Must-know

Items that are shown get clicks, which makes them look better, so they are shown more: the popularity feedback loop. New or niche items never get the exposure to prove themselves. Fixes: reserve exploration traffic and use bandits — ε-greedy (explore at random ε of the time), UCB, or Thompson sampling (sample each item's CTR from its posterior and show the best sample: uncertain items get tried, proven losers fade). Add diversity in re-ranking (MMR), track coverage and long-tail exposure, and correct training for position and exposure bias. Choose the reward carefully — clicks are fast but noisy; purchases are slower but what you want.
