MODULE 02 · EVALUATION

Evaluation metrics

Every classifier outputs a score; a threshold turns it into a decision. Precision, recall, F1, ROC and PR curves are all just different views of where you put that threshold — and of how many positives exist in the first place. Interviewers want you to pick the metric from the cost of each error, not from habit.

Concept

Precision: "when I say positive, am I right?" Recall: "of all positives, how many did I catch?" Must-know

\( \text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP} \qquad \text{Recall (TPR)}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} \qquad F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R} \qquad \text{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN} \)

Raising the threshold makes the model more conservative: fewer false positives (precision ↑) but more missed positives (recall ↓). Choose by cost: missing cancer or fraud is expensive → favour recall; flagging a legitimate email as spam or blocking a good customer is expensive → favour precision. F1 is the harmonic mean, so it is only high when both are.

Concept

ROC-AUC barely notices imbalance; PR-AUC does Must-know

The ROC curve plots TPR vs FPR over all thresholds; AUC is the probability that a random positive scores above a random negative (0.5 = random, 1 = perfect). Both axes are rates within a class, so ROC is unchanged by prevalence. The PR curve plots precision vs recall; precision depends on how many negatives there are, so when positives are rare (fraud, ads clicks, disease) PR-AUC drops sharply while ROC-AUC still looks great. Baseline PR-AUC = prevalence, not 0.5.

Concept

Accuracy lies when one class is rare Must-know

With 1% fraud, a model that says "not fraud" for everyone is 99% accurate and useless. Fix it at three levels: metrics (PR-AUC, recall at a fixed precision, F1, cost-weighted metrics), training (class weights, focal loss, under-/over-sampling, SMOTE — inside CV folds only), and decision (tune the threshold on validation data). Keep validation and test sets at the real-world ratio.

Concept

MSE punishes big errors, MAE treats every unit the same

\( \text{MSE}=\tfrac1n\sum(y-\hat y)^2 \quad \text{RMSE}=\sqrt{\text{MSE}} \quad \text{MAE}=\tfrac1n\sum|y-\hat y| \quad R^2 = 1-\frac{\sum(y-\hat y)^2}{\sum(y-\bar y)^2} \)

Squaring makes MSE/RMSE dominated by a few large errors and outliers (and minimising MSE predicts the mean); MAE is robust and minimising it predicts the median. RMSE is in the target's units. R² is the share of variance explained versus predicting the mean — it can be negative. MAPE breaks near zero targets. Drag the outlier.

Concept

A calibrated 0.8 means "right 80% of the time"

Ranking metrics (AUC) only care about order. When the probability itself drives a decision — expected revenue = p(click) × bid in ads, risk pricing, combining models — it must be calibrated. Check with a reliability diagram (predicted vs observed rate per bin), the Brier score or log-loss, and fix with Platt scaling (a logistic fit on the scores) or isotonic regression on a held-out set. Boosted trees and over-trained nets tend to be overconfident; class re-weighting or downsampling shifts probabilities and needs correcting.
