No labels: find structure. k-means is the clustering question every interviewer asks ("walk me through it, how do you pick k, when does it fail?"), and PCA is the dimensionality-reduction one ("what are the principal components, and why scale first?").

Concept

Assign to nearest centroid, move centroid to the mean, repeat Must-know

\( \min_{C_1..C_k}\ \sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{x\in C_j}\lVert x-\mu_j\rVert^2 \) (within-cluster sum of squares, WCSS)

Each iteration can only lower WCSS, so it converges — but to a local minimum that depends on the starting centroids. k-means++ picks initial centroids far apart (probability ∝ squared distance), and running several restarts (n_init) keeps the best. k-means assumes roughly spherical, similar-sized clusters and needs scaled features.

Concept

WCSS always falls with k — look for the elbow, and check silhouette Must-know

Adding clusters always reduces within-cluster distance (k = n gives zero), so you can't just minimise it. The elbow method looks for where extra clusters stop paying off. The silhouette score \(s=(b-a)/\max(a,b)\) compares each point's distance to its own cluster (a) with the nearest other cluster (b): close to 1 is good, near 0 means overlapping clusters. Also use the gap statistic, BIC for GMMs, stability across resamples — and whether the clusters are useful for the business.

Concept

The first principal component is the direction of maximum variance Must-know

PCA finds orthogonal directions (eigenvectors of the covariance matrix) ordered by how much variance they capture (eigenvalues). Projecting onto the top k components keeps most of the information in fewer dimensions and removes correlation between features. Maximising variance along an axis is the same as minimising the squared distance of points to it. Always standardise first, or features with big units dominate; fit PCA on training data only.

\( \Sigma=\tfrac1n X_c^\top X_c = V\Lambda V^\top,\qquad \text{explained ratio}_i=\lambda_i/\textstyle\sum_j\lambda_j \)
